## The chapter and its place

The divisibility half of Theorem A works with theta functions whose values are algebraic integers. The vacuum vector of the Weil representation is the natural starting point, but its values are only algebraic numbers: they are integral up to a scalar that nobody computes. This chapter finds such a scalar. It builds the *integral vacuum* $\theta^\circ$, a multiple of the vacuum whose values at the finite points are integral at every prime, whose conjugates are bounded, and which is not zero. It also builds the *standard data* at a prime $p$, the module over the $p$-integral complex numbers that later chapters test for divisibility. The tool is old and simple: a product of theta functions of a CM elliptic curve, composed with linear forms that make up a *tight frame*.

The plan of Theorem A is in [Theorem A and its plan](/chapter/theorem-a-plan/), and the place of Theorem A in the whole argument is in [the theorem and the plan](/chapter/theorem-and-plan/). The field $E$, its different and its Weil data at the primes are in [the field E at the primes](/chapter/field-at-primes/). The chapters that use this one are [the CM curve, its formal group and the normal form](/chapter/cm-formal-group/), [the count and the divisibility of standard data](/chapter/normal-forms/) and [theta kernels and gauges](/chapter/theta-kernels-gauges/). The frame of length $M$ in Section 7 is used by [the divisibility is sharp](/chapter/sharpness/) in Volume 11.

### The setting

Fix parameters $P$: a modulus $q_0$ with $18480 \mid q_0$ and a prime $d \equiv q_0/2 - 1 \pmod{q_0}$, so that $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$. Let $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ and $\beta = (1 + \sqrt{-d})/2$, so that $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\beta]$ and $\beta\bar\beta = (d+1)/4$. For $s \ge 0$ the field $E = K(\zeta)$ is generated by a primitive root of unity $\zeta$ of order $q_1 = q_0 \cdot 3^{\lfloor \log_3 s \rfloor + 5} \cdot 2^s$. It is a CM field. Its infinite places $\tau$ form a group isomorphic to $\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$, and for each place the CM type gives an embedding $\tau \colon E \to \mathbb{C}$ that is the identity on $K$. There are $f = \varphi(q_1)$ places. These objects are in [CM fields](/chapter/cm-fields/) and [the field E at the primes](/chapter/field-at-primes/). For $x, y \in E$ write

$$\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_\tau \tau(x)\, \overline{\tau(y)},$$

and identify $y$ with the vector $(\tau(y))_\tau$ of $\mathbb{C}^f$. The Weil data of $E$ give a finite idele $\alpha$. It is $1/P'(\zeta)$ at the primes that divide $\beta$ and $1$ elsewhere, where $P'(\zeta)$ is the value at $\zeta$ of the derivative of the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_{q_1}$. The lattice

$$\mathfrak{a}_0 = E \cap \alpha \hat{\mathcal{O}}$$

is the fractional ideal of $\alpha$, and $\mathfrak{a}_0 \bar{\mathfrak{a}}_0$ is the inverse different of $E/K$ ([the field E at the primes](/chapter/field-at-primes/)). The lattice $\mathfrak{a}_0^n$ of rational vectors appears in the theta functions of rank $n$.

For a hermitian space $V$ of rank $n$ over $E$ the Weil representation acts on a space $\mathcal{S}(V)$ with a vacuum vector $\mathrm{vac}$ and a theta functional $\theta$ ([the Weil representation](/chapter/weil-representation/)). A vector $f$ is read as the function $f(y) = \theta(\varrho(y) f)$ of adelic vectors $y$, where $\varrho$ is the Heisenberg action. The points that matter are the *finite points* $\mathrm{fin}(x)$, with finite part $\alpha x$ and real part $0$.

### What the chapter proves

The chapter proves three things. Each is a statement of the source, given exactly in the sections named.

1. *Integral vacuum.* There is a nonzero scalar $c_s$ for each $s$ such that, in a standard space of rank $n$, the vector $\theta^\circ = c_s^n \cdot \mathrm{vac}$ has values at finite points that are integral at every $p$-adic place of $\mathbb{C}$. Their conjugates under every automorphism of $\mathbb{C}$ have modulus at most $\exp(C q_1^2)^n$, with a constant $C$ that does not depend on $s$. Some value is not zero (Section 5).
2. *Conjugate jets.* The automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$ over $K$ act on the Taylor series of the translates of one vector $F$, taken at the scale $\mathrm{jetScale}$, through a change of scale to a number of modulus $\Omega_E^{-1/2}$, with $\Omega_E = e^{C_d}\,2\pi/\mathrm{rd}(E)$ for the Chowla-Selberg constant $C_d$ and the root discriminant $\mathrm{rd}(E)$, and a permutation of the places (Section 6).
3. *Short frames.* There are tight frames of every length $M = p_0p_1$ of the data, with large multiplicities and a vector whose linear form is a unit at a given prime (Section 7).

The definitions that hold all this together are those of a tight frame and of the scale.

**Definition (Tight frame).** Let $w_i \in E$ for $i$ in a finite set $I$, and let $m_i \in \mathbb{N}$ and $M \in \mathbb{N}$. The family $(w_i)$ with multiplicities $(m_i)$ is a *tight frame of length* $M$ if every $w_i$ lies in $\mathfrak{a}_0$ and
$$\sum_{i \in I} m_i\, \sigma(w_i)\, \overline{\tau(w_i)} = M\, \delta_{\sigma\tau} \qquad \text{for all infinite places } \sigma, \tau.$$

Lean: `New.IsTightFrame` (Lib/General/LinearAlgebra/PolarDecomposition.lean).

In matrix terms, let $W$ be the $f \times I$ matrix with entries $\sigma(w_i)$ and $D$ the diagonal matrix of the $m_i$. The condition says $W D W^* = M \cdot 1$. Equivalently, $\sum_i m_i \langle z, w_i\rangle\, w_i = M z$ for every $z \in \mathbb{C}^f$, where $\langle z, w \rangle = \sum_\tau z_\tau \overline{\tau(w)}$. Taking the inner product with $z$ gives
$$\sum_i m_i\, |\langle z, w_i \rangle|^2 = M\, |z|^2 .$$
So the $M$ linear forms $z \mapsto \langle z, w_i \rangle$, each counted $m_i$ times, add up the standard hermitian form. Sets of vectors with this property are called eutactic stars in the theory of lattices.

**Remark (Formalization).** The source puts the condition $w_i \in \mathfrak{a}_0$ into the definition of a tight frame. A tight frame in the sense of linear algebra need not lie in $\mathfrak{a}_0$. The length $M$ and the multiplicities are natural numbers, and nothing forbids $M = 0$; then all $w_i$ with $m_i > 0$ are $0$. The definition sits in a file on polar decompositions, but it does not use them.

### A model case

Pretend for one paragraph that $E = K$, so that $f = 1$, and take $n = 1$. Let $C$ be the CM lattice $\Omega_0 \mathcal{O}_K$ of [CM elliptic curves](/chapter/cm-elliptic-curves/), with theta function $\theta_C$ of degree one. The function $z \mapsto \theta_C(\Omega_0 z / \sqrt{2\pi/\sqrt d})$ has the transformation law of a theta function in one variable for the lattice of the space $V$ of rank one. By Fourier inversion it is the theta function of a vector $F$. The values of $F$ at finite points are values of $\theta_C$ at torsion points, times a Gaussian factor. These are the torsion values $\theta_C[u]$, and they are integral by [the theorem on monomials](/decl/New.CMLattice.hasIntegralValues_monomialSection/). Fourier inversion also writes a nonzero multiple of $\mathrm{vac}$ as a finite sum of translates of $F$ with root of unity coefficients. So that multiple has integral values.

For the true field $E$ there are $f > 1$ variables, and one function of one variable is not enough. A product $\prod_i \theta_C(\ell_i(z))^{m_i}$ over linear forms $\ell_i$ has the Gaussian factor of the hermitian form $\sum_i m_i |\ell_i(z)|^2$, up to a constant. That form must be the standard one, so the forms must make up a tight frame. The forms must also take the lattice $\mathfrak{a}_0^n$ into the periods of $C$, which is why the frame vectors lie in $\mathfrak{a}_0$. The simplest frame is the family of powers $\zeta^k$, $0 \le k < q_1$, of length $q_1$.

### The sketch

The chapter has seven more sections.

- Section 2 treats tight frames: the lattice $\mathfrak{a}_0$, the integral pairing on it, the frame of powers of $\zeta$, and the operations that make new frames.
- Section 3 fixes the scale. It defines the CM curve, the unit of Chowla and Selberg, the period factor and the scale $\mathrm{jetScale}$, and computes their absolute values at the $p$-adic places.
- Section 4 builds the theta function of a product of monomial sections along a tight frame, and proves that its values are integral.
- Section 5 applies this to the powers of $\zeta$ and builds the integral vacuum $\theta^\circ$ and the standard data.
- Section 6 computes the conjugates of the jets of the translates of one vector.
- Section 7 builds the short frames: a frame of a length $h$ prime to $q_1$ with $h \le b$, and frames of every large length.
- Section 8 has exercises and notes.

The integral vacuum is a definition of the source:

**Definition (Integral vacuum).** For a space $V$ of rank $n$, $\theta^\circ = c_s^{\,n} \cdot \mathrm{vac}$, with the scalar $c_s = \mathrm{vacuumScale}$ of Section 5.
