## Semi-invariants, minors and the Fock pairing

This section sets up the objects of the chapter: arrays of variables, weights as counts of columns, the module of Borel semi-invariants, products of minors, the substitution of rows and the Fock pairing. All of them are in `Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean`.

### The textbook object

Let $K$ be a field of characteristic $0$, let $n \le N$, and let $M = M_{n \times N}(K)$ be the space of $n \times N$ matrices. The group $GL_n \times GL_N$ acts on the polynomial functions on $M$ by $(g, h) f (y) = f(g^{\mathsf T} y h)$. Let $B$ be the group of upper triangular matrices in $GL_N$ and $U$ its unipotent radical. The classical theorem of the first fundamental theorem type says that the ring $K[M]^U$ of the polynomials invariant under right translation by $U$ is generated by the leading minors $\det(y_{I, \{1, \dots, h\}})$, for $h \le n$ and $I$ a set of $h$ rows. The torus of $B$ acts on $K[M]^U$, and its eigenspaces are the irreducible representations of $GL_n$: the eigenspace for the character $\operatorname{diag}(t) \mapsto \prod_i t_i^{\lambda_i}$ is the polynomial representation of highest weight $\lambda$, for every partition $\lambda$ with at most $n$ parts. In the language of algebraic groups this eigenspace is the induced module $H^0(\lambda)$ of Jantzen, *Representations of Algebraic Groups*, 2nd ed. 2003, Part II, Chapter 2, and over $\mathbb Z$ it is the Schur module of Akin, Buchsbaum and Weyman, Adv. Math. 44 (1982). The source uses only the eigenspace, and takes it as the definition.

### Arrays and weights

**Definition.** For a type of rows $m$ and a commutative ring $R$, the ring of *array polynomials* is the polynomial ring over $R$ in the variables $X(a, i)$, with a row $a \in m$ and a column $i \in \mathbb N$.

Lean: `New.MatrixPoly` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The columns are indexed by all of $\mathbb N$. A polynomial uses only finitely many of them. So the number $N$ of the textbook does not appear, and no statement of the chapter has to fix it.

The source works with several places at once. Let $\Phi$ be a finite set, the set of places. At each place $\tau$ there is a finite type of rows $n_\tau$, and $|n_\tau|$ is its number of elements.

Lean: `New.rankAt` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The rows of the whole array are the pairs $(\tau, a)$ with $a \in n_\tau$. A row $(\tau, a)$ has the *colour* $\tau$. A weight is a function $\kappa$ with $\kappa_\tau(h) \in \mathbb N$: the number of columns of height $h$ in the Young diagram at $\tau$. Only the heights $0 < h < |n_\tau|$ count. The columns of full height $|n_\tau|$ are the powers of the determinant. The source treats them by a twist with $\det^{e}$ (Section 4).

**Definition.** For bounds $N_\tau$ and a weight $\kappa$, the *diagram weight* is the finitely supported function on $\Phi \times \mathbb N$ with

$$\lambda(\tau, i) = \sum_{i < h < N_\tau} \kappa_\tau(h).$$

Lean: `New.diagramWeight` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The number $\lambda(\tau, i)$ is the number of columns that are higher than $i$, that is, the length of row $i + 1$ of the diagram at $\tau$, with rows counted from $1$. So $\lambda(\tau, \cdot)$ is the partition of the diagram. For example, let $N = 3$, $\kappa(1) = 2$ and $\kappa(2) = 1$. The diagram has one column of height $2$ and two of height $1$, so it is the partition $(3, 1)$. The definition gives $\lambda(0) = \kappa(1) + \kappa(2) = 3$, $\lambda(1) = \kappa(2) = 1$ and $\lambda(2) = 0$.

### Borel semi-invariants

**Definition.** Let $c$ colour the rows of an array. For an array $b(\tau, i, j)$ and an array $y(a, j)$, the product $y b$ is the array with entries

$$(y b)(a, j) = \sum_{i \le j} y(a, i)\, b(c(a), i, j).$$

Lean: `New.borelMul` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

So $yb$ is $y$ times an upper triangular matrix $b_\tau$, with one matrix for each colour, acting on the columns. Only the entries with $i \le j$ are used.

**Definition.** For a colouring $c$ and a weight $l$ on $\Phi \times \mathbb N$, the module $\nabla(l)$ of *Borel semi-invariants* is the set of array polynomials $f$ over $R$ with

$$f(y b) = \prod_{(\tau, i)} b(\tau, i, i)^{l(\tau, i)} \cdot f(y)$$

for the generic upper triangular arrays $b$, that is, as an identity of polynomials in the variables $y$ and $b$.

Lean: `New.nabla` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The generic form implies the form at every point, in every algebra. The source proves the equivalence.

**Lemma.** A polynomial $f$ lies in $\nabla(l)$ if and only if for every commutative $R$-algebra $S$ and all arrays $b$ and $y$ over $S$, $f(yb) = \prod b(\tau, i, i)^{l(\tau, i)} f(y)$.

Lean: `New.mem_nabla` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

*Proof.* The generic identity is an identity of polynomials, so it holds after every evaluation into $S$. Conversely, take $S$ to be the polynomial ring over $R$ in the entries of $b$ and $y$, and the generic arrays. $\square$

The textbook definition uses invariance under the unipotent group and an eigenvalue for the torus. The definition of the source combines them into one identity for the whole Borel group. Over a ring that is not a field, this is the right definition: it does not need the group to be reduced or the ring to be a domain.

### Minors

**Definition.** For an $h$-tuple $I$ of rows, the *minor* $\Delta_I$ is the determinant of the $h \times h$ matrix of the variables $X(I_s, t)$, for $s, t < h$, in the rows $I$ and the first $h$ columns.

Lean: `New.minor` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

**Lemma.** Let $I$ be an $h$-tuple of rows of colour $\tau$. Then $\Delta_I$ lies in $\nabla(l)$ for the weight $l$ that is $1$ at $(\tau, 0), \dots, (\tau, h - 1)$ and $0$ elsewhere.

Lean: `New.minor_mem_nabla` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

*Proof.* Let $Y$ be the $h \times h$ matrix $y(I_s, t)$. The rows $I$ of $yb$ in the first $h$ columns form the matrix $Y B$, where $B$ is the upper left $h \times h$ block of $b_\tau$. This uses that $b_\tau$ is upper triangular: the column $t < h$ of $yb$ uses only the columns $i \le t$ of $y$. So $\Delta_I(yb) = \det(Y) \det(B) = \prod_{i < h} b(\tau, i, i) \cdot \Delta_I(y)$. $\square$

A product of semi-invariants is a semi-invariant, and the weights add. So a product of minors, one for each column of a diagram, has the diagram weight.

**Definition.** A *filling* $T$ of a diagram with $k(h)$ columns of height $h < N$ assigns to each column of height $h$ an $h$-tuple of rows. There is no condition of order. The *minor product* of $T$ is $\prod_{\text{columns } C} \Delta_{T(C)}$.

Lean: `New.tableauPoly` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean). The type of fillings is `New.Tableau`.

**Lemma.** Let $T_\tau$ be a filling with rows in $n_\tau$ of the diagram $\kappa_\tau$ with heights below $M_\tau$, for each $\tau$. Then $\prod_\tau \operatorname{minorProduct}(T_\tau)$ lies in $\nabla(\lambda)$, where $\lambda$ is the diagram weight of $\kappa$ with the bounds $M$.

Lean: `New.prod_tableauPoly_mem_nabla` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/SchurMatrix.lean).

**Definition.** The *module of semi-invariants of weight $\kappa$* is $\nabla(\lambda)$ for the colouring of the row $(\tau, a)$ by $\tau$ and the diagram weight $\lambda$ of $\kappa$ with the bounds $|n_\tau|$.

Lean: `New.nablaFamily` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The Weyl module of the chapter is the dual of this module. Section 3 gives it a basis of minor products. Then the Weyl module is the space of coordinate vectors in the dual basis.

### Substitution of rows

**Definition.** For matrices $z_\tau$ of size $n'_\tau \times n_\tau$ over $R$, the *row substitution* is the $R$-algebra map from the array polynomials in the rows $(\tau, b)$, $b \in n'_\tau$, to those in the rows $(\tau, a)$, $a \in n_\tau$, that sends $X((\tau, b), i)$ to $\sum_a z_\tau(b, a) X((\tau, a), i)$. In short, $f \mapsto f(zy)$.

Lean: `New.rowSubst` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

**Lemma.** If $f$ is a semi-invariant of weight $l$ in the rows $n'$, then $f(zy)$ is a semi-invariant of weight $l$ in the rows $n$.

Lean: `New.rowSubst_mem_nabla` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

*Proof.* Left multiplication by $z$ and right multiplication by $b$ commute: $(zy)b = z(yb)$. Apply the semi-invariance of $f$ at the array $zy$, by the pointwise form of the definition. $\square$

The matrices $z_\tau$ need not be square or invertible. This is used in Section 4 to compare Weyl modules of different ranks.

### The Fock pairing

**Definition.** The *Fock pairing* of two polynomials $p$ and $q$ over $R$ in any set of variables is $\langle p, q\rangle = p(\partial) q (0)$. In coordinates: if $p = \sum_d p_d x^d$ and $q = \sum_d q_d x^d$, then $\langle p, q \rangle = \sum_d d!\, p_d q_d$, where $d! = \prod_j d_j!$.

Lean: `New.fockPairing` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

The source defines it through a pairing of polynomials with power series (`New.jet`), but the formula is the one above. Monomials are orthogonal, and $\langle x^d, x^d\rangle = d!$.

**Lemma.** The Fock pairing is symmetric.

Lean: `New.fockPairing_comm` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

**Lemma.** Let $p$ be a polynomial in the rows $n'$ and $q$ a polynomial in the rows $n$, and let $z_\tau$ be $n'_\tau \times n_\tau$ matrices. Then $\langle p(zy), q \rangle = \langle p, q(z^{\mathsf T} y)\rangle$.

Lean: `New.fockPairing_rowSubst` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

*Proof.* Both sides are bilinear in $p$ and $q$. The pairing and the substitution both respect the splitting of the variables by columns: the pairing of two products over the columns is the product of the pairings, and the substitution acts on each column separately. So it suffices to treat the variables of one column. Then it suffices to treat the case where $p$ and $q$ are products of linear forms, because powers of linear forms span the polynomials over $\mathbb Q$, and the identity is an identity of polynomials with integer coefficients in the entries of $z$. For linear forms $u$ and $v$, $\langle u^d, v^{d'} \rangle = 0$ if $d \ne d'$ and $d! (u \cdot v)^d$ if $d = d'$, where $u \cdot v$ is the dot product of the coefficient vectors. The substitution replaces the coefficient vector $u$ of $p$ by $z^{\mathsf T} u$, so the left side has the factor $(z^{\mathsf T} u \cdot v)^d$ and the right side has $(u \cdot z v)^d$. These are equal. $\square$

The source proves it differently, through the chain rule for a linear substitution (`New.pderiv_linSubst`) and the adjointness of multiplication by $X_i$ and the derivative $\partial / \partial X_i$. The lemma says that the adjoint of the action of $z$ for the Fock pairing is the action of $z^{\mathsf T}$. For complex coefficients and unitary $z$, the adjoint of $z$ for the Hermitian form $\langle \bar p, q\rangle$ is then $z^{-1}$. This is the root of the unitarity in Section 5.

**Lemma.** Let $R$ be a star ordered domain, for example $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. Then $\langle \bar p, p \rangle > 0$ for every nonzero polynomial $p$, where $\bar p$ applies the star to each coefficient.

Lean: `New.fockPairing_star_self_pos` (Lib/GLn/SchurModule/Basic.lean).

*Proof.* $\langle \bar p, p\rangle = \sum_d d!\, \overline{p_d} p_d$, a sum of nonnegative terms, and the term of a $d$ with $p_d \ne 0$ is positive. $\square$

So over $\mathbb C$ the Fock pairing gives a positive definite Hermitian form on every space of polynomials. Its restriction to the semi-invariants of weight $\kappa$ is an invariant form for the compact group. Section 5 compares it with the Gram form of the kernel.
