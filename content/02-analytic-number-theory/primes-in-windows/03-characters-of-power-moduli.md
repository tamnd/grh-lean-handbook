## Characters of power moduli

This section proves the first two steps of the plan. The first is algebraic: for $4 \mid Q$ and $k \geq 2$, every character $\chi$ modulo $Q^k$ with $\chi^2 = 1$ is induced from a character modulo $Q^2$, and it has the same L-function. The second is analytic: for every large $X$, no L-function of a character modulo $Q^k \leq X^A$ vanishes on the disc $|s - 1| \leq 1/X$, and its logarithmic derivative is at most $X$ there. Sections 5 and 6 use the second result at the centre of Newman's argument.

### The problem of the real characters

Let $q = Q^k$. The proof of Section 4 needs, for every character $\chi$ modulo $q$, a disc about $s = 1$ on which $L(s, \chi)$ has no zero, and a bound for $L'/L$ on it. The radius may shrink with $q$, but only like a power of $\log q$. For the characters with $\chi^2 \neq 1$ the classical $3$-$4$-$1$ argument gives this, uniformly in $q$. The reason is that the argument uses $L(\sigma + 2it, \chi^2)$, and this function has no pole when $\chi^2$ is not principal. For the real characters, those with $\chi^2 = 1$, the function $L(s, \chi^2)$ has a pole at $s = 1$, and the argument fails near $s = 1$. This is the source of the possible exceptional zero of Section 2.

A character $\chi$ with values in $\mathbb{C}$ is real if and only if $\chi^2 = 1$, since its values are $0$ and roots of unity, and the only real roots of unity are $\pm 1$. In this section "real" means $\chi^2 = 1$. The principal character is real.

### A model case: powers of $4$

Take $Q = 4$. The group $(\mathbb{Z}/4^k\mathbb{Z})^\times = (\mathbb{Z}/2^{2k}\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is the product of $\{\pm 1\}$ and the cyclic group generated by $5$, which has order $2^{2k-2}$. A character with $\chi^2 = 1$ is fixed by the two signs $\chi(-1)$ and $\chi(5)$. So there are exactly four real characters modulo $4^k$, for every $k \geq 2$. They are the principal character and the characters induced from $\chi_{-4}$, $\chi_8$ and $\chi_{-8}$, of conductors $4$, $8$ and $8$. All of them come from the modulus $16 = Q^2$. So as $k$ grows, the number of characters modulo $4^k$ grows like $4^k$, but the real ones are always the same four.

The hypothesis $4 \mid Q$ cannot be dropped in this form. For $Q = 2$ and $k = 3$ the character $\chi_8$ modulo $8$ is real, but it does not come from the modulus $Q^2 = 4$, since $\chi_8(5) = -1$ and $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

For general $Q$ with $4 \mid Q$, a real primitive character has a conductor that divides $8\prod_{p \mid Q,\ p \text{ odd}} p$. This number divides $Q^2$, since $16 \mid Q^2$. The source does not use the structure of the groups of units. It proves the needed fact directly, by Hensel's lemma.

### Real characters come from $Q^2$

**Lemma (Real characters come from $Q^2$).** Let $Q \geq 1$ with $4 \mid Q$, let $k \geq 2$, and let $\chi$ be a Dirichlet character modulo $Q^k$ with values in $\mathbb{C}$ and $\chi^2 = 1$. Then $\chi$ factors through $Q^2$: there is a character $\chi'$ modulo $Q^2$ such that $\chi$ is the character modulo $Q^k$ induced from $\chi'$.
Lean: `New.factorsThrough_sq_of_sq_eq_one` (Lib/Analytic/Primes.lean), with `DirichletCharacter.FactorsThrough` (Mathlib).

*Proof.* Since $Q^2 \mid Q^k$, a character modulo $Q^k$ factors through $Q^2$ if and only if it is trivial on the kernel of the reduction $(\mathbb{Z}/Q^k\mathbb{Z})^\times \to (\mathbb{Z}/Q^2\mathbb{Z})^\times$. So let $u$ be a unit modulo $Q^k$ with $u \equiv 1 \pmod{Q^2}$. We show that $u$ is a square of a unit. Then $\chi(u) = \chi(v)^2 = \chi^2(v) = 1$, as required.

Write $Q = 4m$ and $1 - u = Q^2 t$ with $t \in \mathbb{Z}$. In $R = \mathbb{Z}/Q^k\mathbb{Z}$ consider the polynomial
$$P(c) = c^2 + c + Qmt.$$
If $P(c) = 0$, then
$$(1 + 2c)^2 = 1 + 4(c^2 + c) = 1 - 4Qmt = 1 - Q^2 t = u.$$
So it is enough to find a root $c \in R$ of $P$. The constant term $Qmt$ is nilpotent in $R$, since $(Qmt)^k$ is divisible by $Q^k$. The derivative $P'(0) = 1$ is a unit. Hensel's lemma for a nilpotent defect gives a root: the Newton iteration $c_0 = 0$, $c_{j+1} = c_j - P(c_j)/P'(c_j)$ is well defined, since $P'(c_j) = 1 + 2c_j$ is a unit when $c_j$ is nilpotent, and $P(c_j)$ lies in the $2^j$-th power of the ideal generated by $Qmt$. So $c_j$ is constant for $2^j \geq k$, and its value $c$ is a root. The element $v = 1 + 2c$ is a unit, since $v^2 = u$ is a unit. $\square$

**Example.** Take $Q = 4$ and $k = 3$, so $Q^k = 64$ and $Q^2 = 16$. The units $u \equiv 1 \pmod{16}$ are $1$, $17$, $33$ and $49$. The squares of the odd numbers modulo $64$ are $1^2 = 1$, $3^2 = 9$, $5^2 = 25$, $7^2 = 49$, $9^2 = 81 \equiv 17$, $11^2 = 121 \equiv 57$, $13^2 = 169 \equiv 41$ and $15^2 = 225 \equiv 33$. All four units are squares. For $Q = 2$ and $k = 3$ the unit $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is not a square modulo $8$, since every odd square is $\equiv 1 \pmod 8$. This is the failure of the model case.

The step that needs $4 \mid Q$ is the identity $1 - Q^2 t = 1 - 4Qmt$, which makes the defect a multiple of $4$, so that the unit $u$ has the form $1 + 4(\cdot)$ with a nilpotent term in the bracket.

### The same L-function

The analytic statements below use a second normalization of $L(s, \chi)$. Write $L^\ast(s, \chi)$ for the entire function $\tilde L(s, \chi)$ of [Dirichlet L-functions and zero symmetry](/chapter/dirichlet-l-functions/#characters-and-l-functions): it is $L(s, \chi)$ if $\chi$ is not principal, and the continuation of $(s - 1)L(s, \chi)$ if $\chi$ is principal. Volume 1 writes $L^\ast$, and this chapter keeps that letter.

**Definition (The regular L-function).** Let $\chi$ be a Dirichlet character modulo $N \geq 1$. Put
$$\mathsf{f}_\chi(s) = \begin{cases} L^\ast(s, \chi)/s & \text{if } \chi \text{ is principal}, \\ L^\ast(s, \chi) & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
Lean: `New.regularL` (Lib/Analytic/PrimeNumberTheorem/LFunctionGrowth.lean), with `New.entireL` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

So $\mathsf{f}_\chi(s) = L(s, \chi)$ for $\chi$ not principal, and $\mathsf{f}_\chi(s) = \frac{s - 1}{s}L(s, \chi)$ for $\chi$ principal and $s \neq 0, 1$. The factor $1/s$ keeps the size of $\mathsf{f}_\chi$ comparable to that of $L$ for large $|s|$. At $s = 1$ and principal $\chi$, $\mathsf{f}_\chi(1) = L^\ast(1, \chi) = \varphi(N)/N$.

**Lemma (Same L-function).** Let $Q \geq 1$ with $4 \mid Q$, let $k \geq 2$, and let $\chi$ be a character modulo $Q^k$ with $\chi^2 = 1$. There is a character $\chi'$ modulo $Q^2$ with $\mathsf{f}_\chi = \mathsf{f}_{\chi'}$ as functions on $\mathbb{C}$.
Lean: `New.exists_regularL_eq_of_sq_eq_one` (Lib/Analytic/Primes.lean).

*Proof.* Take $\chi'$ from the lemma above on real characters. Then $\chi$ is principal if and only if $\chi'$ is principal. For an induced character the L-functions differ by the Euler factors at the primes that divide the new modulus but not the old one:
$$L(s, \chi) = L(s, \chi')\prod_{p \mid Q^k,\ p \nmid Q^2}\bigl(1 - \chi'(p)p^{-s}\bigr),$$
for $s \neq 1$ or $\chi'$ not principal. The moduli $Q^k$ and $Q^2$ have the same prime factors, since $k \geq 1$. So the product is empty, and $L(s, \chi) = L(s, \chi')$. For principal characters the values at $s = 1$ are the residues $\prod_{p \mid Q}(1 - 1/p)$, which agree for the same reason. So $L^\ast(\cdot, \chi) = L^\ast(\cdot, \chi')$, and $\mathsf{f}_\chi = \mathsf{f}_{\chi'}$. $\square$

### A disc about 1 for one character

For a single character, the non-vanishing at $s = 1$ and continuity give a lower bound on a small disc.

**Lemma (A lower bound near 1).** Let $\chi$ be a character modulo $N \geq 1$. There is $r_0(\chi) > 0$ such that for every real $r$ with $|r| < r_0(\chi)$ and every $s \in \mathbb{C}$ with $|s - 1| \leq r$,
$$r \leq |\mathsf{f}_\chi(s)|.$$
Lean: `New.eventually_le_norm_regularL` (Lib/Analytic/PrimeNumberTheorem/ZeroFreeRegion.lean).

*Proof.* The value $\mathsf{f}_\chi(1) = L^\ast(1, \chi)$ is not $0$, by the non-vanishing of $L^\ast$ on $\operatorname{Re} s \geq 1$ ([Lemma](/decl/New.entireL_ne_zero/)). The function $\mathsf{f}_\chi$ is holomorphic near $1$. So $|\mathsf{f}_\chi(s)| \geq |\mathsf{f}_\chi(1)|/2$ on a disc $|s - 1| \leq \delta$. Take $r_0 = \min(\delta, |\mathsf{f}_\chi(1)|/2)$. For $r < 0$ the statement is empty. $\square$

The number $r_0(\chi)$ is not explicit. It depends on $|L(1, \chi)|$ and on the size of $L'$ near $1$. For a real character, $L(1, \chi)$ is positive, and the class number formula expresses it by a class number and a regulator. These are fixed numbers once $\chi$ is fixed.

### The zero-free region

The chapter [the prime number theorem in progressions](/chapter/prime-number-theorem/) proves the zero-free region of the source by the $3$-$4$-$1$ inequality. Here is the form that this section uses.

**Theorem (Zero-free region).** Let $\chi$ be a character modulo $N \geq 1$, and let $l \geq 10^{10}$ with $N \leq e^l$. If $\chi^2 = 1$, assume that $|\mathsf{f}_\chi(z)| \geq 1/l$ for every $z$ with $|z - 1| \leq 1/l$. Then for every $s$ with
$$\operatorname{Re} s \geq 1 - l^{-25} \qquad\text{and}\qquad |s| \leq e^l,$$
we have $L^\ast(s, \chi) \neq 0$ and $|(L^\ast)'/L^\ast(s, \chi)| \leq 2l^{25}$.
Lean: `New.norm_logDeriv_entireL_le` (Lib/Analytic/PrimeNumberTheorem/ZeroFreeRegion.lean).

*Proof.* This is proved in [the prime number theorem in progressions](/chapter/prime-number-theorem/). $\square$

The width $l^{-25}$ is much smaller than the classical $c/l$. It is what the $3$-$4$-$1$ inequality gives with crude bounds for $L$ and $L'$ near the line $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$. The hypothesis on real characters is exactly the input that the $3$-$4$-$1$ argument cannot supply by itself. For a fixed character it holds for large $l$ by the [lower bound near 1](/decl/New.eventually_le_norm_regularL/), with $r = 1/l$. For the characters modulo $Q^k$ it holds uniformly in $k$, by the [lemma on the same L-function](/decl/New.exists_regularL_eq_of_sq_eq_one/), since only the finitely many characters modulo $Q^2$ occur.

### No zeros near 1, uniformly in $k$

**Lemma (No zeros near 1).** Let $A \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $Q \geq 1$ with $4 \mid Q$. There is $X_0$ such that for every real $X \geq X_0$ the following holds. For every $k > 1$ with $Q^k \leq X^A$, every character $\chi$ modulo $Q^k$, and every $s \in \mathbb{C}$ with $|s - 1| \leq 1/X$,
$$L^\ast(s, \chi) \neq 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Bigl|\frac{(L^\ast)'}{L^\ast}(s, \chi)\Bigr| \leq X.$$
Lean: `New.eventually_norm_logDeriv_entireL_le_near_one` (Lib/Analytic/Primes.lean).

*Proof.* Put $l = (A + 1)\log X$, so that $e^l = X^{A + 1}$. Take $X_0 \geq 2$ so large that for $X \geq X_0$ four conditions hold:

1. $l \geq 10^{10}$;
2. $2l^{25} \leq X$, which holds for large $X$ since $(\log X)^{25} = o(X)$;
3. $X \geq 2$;
4. $1/l < r_0(\chi')$ for each of the finitely many characters $\chi'$ modulo $Q^2$, where $r_0$ is the radius of the [lower bound near 1](/decl/New.eventually_le_norm_regularL/).

Let $X \geq X_0$, let $k > 1$ with $Q^k \leq X^A$, let $\chi$ be a character modulo $Q^k$, and let $|s - 1| \leq 1/X$. We apply the [zero-free region](/decl/New.norm_logDeriv_entireL_le/) with $N = Q^k$ and this $l$, and check its hypotheses.

- The modulus: $Q^k \leq X^A \leq X^A \cdot X = e^l$, since $X \geq 1$.
- The real part: $|\operatorname{Re} s - 1| \leq |s - 1| \leq 1/X \leq l^{-25}$, by condition 2. So $\operatorname{Re} s \geq 1 - l^{-25}$.
- The size: $|s| \leq 1 + 1/X \leq 2 \leq e^l$.
- The real characters: if $\chi^2 = 1$, the [lemma on the same L-function](/decl/New.exists_regularL_eq_of_sq_eq_one/) gives $\chi'$ modulo $Q^2$ with $\mathsf{f}_\chi = \mathsf{f}_{\chi'}$. By condition 4 and the lower bound near $1$ with $r = 1/l$, $|\mathsf{f}_\chi(z)| = |\mathsf{f}_{\chi'}(z)| \geq 1/l$ for $|z - 1| \leq 1/l$.

The zero-free region gives $L^\ast(s, \chi) \neq 0$ and $|(L^\ast)'/L^\ast(s, \chi)| \leq 2l^{25} \leq X$, by condition 2. $\square$

The threshold $X_0$ has three parts. Condition 1 asks $X \geq \exp(10^{10}/(A + 1))$. Condition 2 asks $2(A + 1)^{25}(\log X)^{25} \leq X$. Condition 4 asks $(A + 1)\log X > 1/r_0(\chi')$ for the characters modulo $Q^2$. The first two are explicit. The third depends on the values of finitely many fixed L-functions near $1$. No step uses Siegel's theorem.

**Remark (Formalization).** Four points matter for the meaning of these statements. First, the lemma on no zeros near $1$ is an eventual statement along `atTop` in $X$. The source does not give $X_0$, and condition 4 is the part that it would have to compute. Second, the source writes `M` for the number $Q$, and `regularL χ` for $\mathsf{f}_\chi$. For principal $\chi$ the Lean definition divides by $s$, and at $s = 0$ Lean's convention $x/0 = 0$ gives $\mathsf{f}_\chi(0) = 0$. The lemmas use $\mathsf{f}_\chi$ only near $s = 1$, so this value does not matter. Third, the hypothesis $\chi^2 = 1$ of the zero-free region includes the principal character, for which the hypothesis on $\mathsf{f}_\chi$ near $1$ holds by the lower bound near $1$ as well. Fourth, the lower bound near $1$ is stated with the filter of neighbourhoods of $0$, so it also speaks of negative $r$, for which it says nothing. The classical form is the statement for $0 < r < r_0$.
