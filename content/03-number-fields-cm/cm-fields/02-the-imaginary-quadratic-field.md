## The imaginary quadratic field K

The model case of a CM field is an imaginary quadratic field. Its maximal real subfield is $\mathbb{Q}$, complex conjugation is its nontrivial automorphism, and it has one infinite place. The field $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ of the argument is of this kind. This section gives the classical facts about $K$ that the source uses, and the form in which the source states them.

### The classical facts

Let $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ be a prime and $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$. Then $-d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. The ring of integers of $K$ is $\mathbb{Z}[\beta_K]$ with $\beta_K = (1 + \sqrt{-d})/2$, and the discriminant of $K$ is $-d$ (Ireland and Rosen, *A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory*, Ch. 13, §1). The element $\beta_K$ is a root of
$$X^2 - X + \frac{d+1}{4},$$
a polynomial with integer coefficients, since $4 \mid d + 1$. Its discriminant is $1 - (d+1) = -d$.

A rational prime $p$ decomposes in $K$ in one of three ways. Either $p \mathcal{O}_K$ is prime of norm $p^2$ (inert), or $p \mathcal{O}_K = w \bar w$ with $w \ne \bar w$ of norm $p$ (split), or $p \mathcal{O}_K = w^2$ with $w$ of norm $p$ (ramified). This is the fundamental identity $\sum e_i f_i = 2$ (Neukirch, *Algebraic Number Theory*, Ch. I, Prop. 8.2). The criterion of Dedekind and Kummer (Neukirch, Ch. I, Prop. 8.3), applied to the polynomial above, decides the case. The prime $d$ ramifies. An odd prime $p \ne d$ splits if $\left(\frac{-d}{p}\right) = 1$ and is inert otherwise, and by quadratic reciprocity $\left(\frac{-d}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{p}{d}\right)$ when $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. The prime $2$ splits if $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, since the polynomial above is then $X^2 - X \equiv X(X-1) \pmod 2$, and it is inert if $d \equiv 3 \pmod 8$.

So for $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, which holds for the parameters, the character $\chi_K = (\cdot / d)$ decides everything: a prime $p$ has a place of degree one above it if and only if $\chi_K(p) \ne -1$.

The different of $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\beta_K]$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the derivative of the minimal polynomial at $\beta_K$ (Neukirch, Ch. III, §2), that is, by $2 \beta_K - 1 = \sqrt{-d}$.

Last, $K$ embeds in the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$. For a primitive $d$-th root of unity $\eta$, the quadratic Gauss sum $g = \sum_{a \bmod d} \left(\frac{a}{d}\right) \eta^a$ satisfies $g^2 = \left(\frac{-1}{d}\right) d = -d$ (Ireland and Rosen, Ch. 6, §3). So $\sqrt{-d} \mapsto g$ defines an embedding.

### The field in the source

**Definition (The field K).** For a natural number $d$, the field $K$ is the subfield $\mathbb{Q}(i \sqrt{d})$ of $\mathbb{C}$.

Lean: `New.QuadField` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

**Remark (Formalization).** The source writes $\sqrt{-d}$ as the complex number $i \sqrt d$, with $\sqrt d$ the real square root. So $K$ is a concrete subfield of $\mathbb{C}$, and its conjugation `star` is the restriction of complex conjugation. The generator is `New.QuadField.sqrtNegD`, with square $-d$. The parameters of Volume 1 use $K$ through the abbreviation `New.Parameters.K`, which is `QuadField d` for the prime $d$ of the parameters.

**Lemma.** For $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, the field $K$ has degree $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.finrank_eq_two` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

*Proof.* The element $i \sqrt d$ is a root of $X^2 + d$. This polynomial has no rational root, since $-d < 0$ is not a rational square. So it is the minimal polynomial, and the degree is $2$. $\square$

The congruence enters only through the class `Fact (d % 4 = 3)`, which gives $d \ne 0$. With the degree, the source records three instances, which have no names. The field $K$ is totally complex, since a real embedding would send $i \sqrt d$ to a real square root of $-d$. It has exactly one infinite place, since a totally complex field of degree $2$ has one. And it is a CM field, by Mathlib's criterion `NumberField.IsCMField.ofCMExtension` (Mathlib) applied to the totally real field $\mathbb{Q}$ and the quadratic extension $K / \mathbb{Q}$.

**Definition (Embedding by a Gauss sum).** Let $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ be a prime, and let $M$ be a field of characteristic $0$ with a primitive $d$-th root of unity $x$. The source defines a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra homomorphism $K \to M$ that sends $i\sqrt d$ to the quadratic Gauss sum of $x$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.embOfPrimitiveRoot` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

**Remark (Formalization).** The square of the Gauss sum is $\chi(-1) d$ by Mathlib's `gaussSum_sq` (Mathlib), and $\chi(-1) = -1$ since $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. So the Gauss sum is a root of $X^2 + d$ in $M$, and the power basis of $K$ lifts to $M$. The source uses this map in one place: the proof that the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_{q_1}$ stays irreducible over $K$ (`New.Parameters.irreducible_cyclotomic`, stated in [the field E](/chapter/fields-places-twists/)). There $M$ is the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$ inside $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, the field `New.cycloField d` of Section 4.

### The ring of integers and the different

**Theorem.** Let $d$ be a prime with $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$. Then the different of $\mathcal{O}_K$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the principal ideal generated by $2 \beta_K - 1 = \sqrt{-d}$, and $\mathbb{Z}[\beta_K] = \mathcal{O}_K$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.different_eq_span_and_adjoin_betaK` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

*Proof.* The element $\beta_K$ is not rational, since $2 \beta_K$ has imaginary part $\sqrt d \ne 0$. As $[K : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$, it generates $K$. It is a root of $f = X^2 - X + (d+1)/4 \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, so it is integral. The discriminant of the order $\mathbb{Z}[\beta_K]$ is the discriminant of $f$, which is $-d$. The index $m = [\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\beta_K]]$ satisfies $-d = m^2 d_K$. As $d$ is prime, $m = 1$. So $\mathbb{Z}[\beta_K] = \mathcal{O}_K$. For a monogenic ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}[\beta]$, the different is generated by $f'(\beta)$, and $f'(\beta_K) = 2 \beta_K - 1$. $\square$

**Remark (Formalization).** The source asks for the class `New.PrimeSevenModEight d`, which says that $d$ is prime and $d \bmod 8 = 7$. The proof uses only $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and that $d$ is prime. The stronger class is the one that the rest of the library carries, so the source states the theorem under it. The source uses the different in the bound $\mathrm{rd}_E \le q_1 d$ of Section 7: the factor $d$ comes from $\sqrt{-d}$.

### The decomposition of primes

**Lemma.** Let $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, let $p$ be a prime, and let $w$ be a finite place of $K$ with $p \in w$. Then one of two things holds.

1. $p \mathcal{O}_K = w$, and $q_w = p^2$.
2. There is a finite place $J$ of $K$ with $p \mathcal{O}_K = w J$, $q_w = p$ and $q_J = p$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.span_eq_or_exists_span_eq_mul` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

*Proof.* As $p \in w$, the ideal $w$ divides $p \mathcal{O}_K$, so $p \mathcal{O}_K = w J$ for an ideal $J$. The absolute norm of $p \mathcal{O}_K$ is $p^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]} = p^2$. So $N(w) N(J) = p^2$ with $N(w) \ne 1$. If $J = \mathcal{O}_K$, then $N(w) = p^2$, which is case 1. Otherwise $N(w) = N(J) = p$. An ideal of prime norm is a prime ideal, so $J$ is a finite place, which is case 2. $\square$

**Remark (Formalization).** In case 2 the source allows $J = w$. This is the ramified case $p = d$. The lemma does not separate split from ramified, and the argument does not need it to. The variant `New.QuadField.prime_inert_or_eq_mul` adds that the places above $p$ are exactly $w$ and $J$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.prime_inert_or_eq_mul` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

**Theorem.** Let $d$ be a prime with $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, let $p$ be a prime, and let $w$ be a finite place of $K$ with $p \in w$. Then $q_w = p$ if and only if $\chi_K(p) \ne -1$.

Lean: `New.QuadField.q_eq_iff_chiK_ne_neg_one` (Lib/General/NumberTheory/ImaginaryQuadraticField.lean).

*Proof.* The residue field $\mathcal{O}_K / w$ has $q_w = p^{f}$ elements, with $f \in \{1, 2\}$ the residue degree, and $q_w = p$ if and only if the Frobenius $y \mapsto y^p$ is the identity on it. The residue field is generated over $\mathbb{F}_p$ by the image $b$ of $\beta_K$. So $q_w = p$ if and only if $b^p = b$, that is, if and only if $f$ has a root in $\mathbb{F}_p$. For $p = 2$, $f \equiv X(X - 1) \pmod 2$ since $(d+1)/4$ is even, so $f$ has a root, and $\chi_K(2) = 1$ since $d \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$. For $p = d$, $f$ has the double root $1/2$, and $\chi_K(d) = 0$. For odd $p \ne d$, the roots of $f$ are $(1 \pm y)/2$ with $y^2 = -d$, so $f$ has a root in $\mathbb{F}_p$ if and only if $-d$ is a square modulo $p$. By quadratic reciprocity and $d \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ this holds if and only if $(p/d) = 1$. $\square$

**Remark (Formalization).** The source proves the odd case through the identity $\chi_K(p) \equiv y^{(p-1)/2}$ in a ring of characteristic $p$ with $y^2 = -d$ (`New.QuadField.chiK_eq_pow_of_sq_eq_neg`), and the case $p = 2$ through the lemma `New.QuadField.chiK_two` that $\chi_K(2) = 1$ for $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$. The theorem holds also for $p = d$, where $\chi_K(d) = 0 \ne -1$ and $w = (\sqrt{-d})$ has norm $d$. So it is stated with no hypothesis on $p$ other than that it is prime.

The theorem is where the congruence $d \equiv 7 \pmod 8$ is used for $K$. With it, every prime $p \mid q_1$ splits in $K$, since $\chi_K(p) = 1$ for such $p$ (`New.Parameters.chiK_eq_one_of_dvd`, in [the parameters](/chapter/fields-places-twists/)). The argument needs primes of degree one in $K$ above each prime of $q_1$, and it needs $2$ to split in particular.
